## Introduction

<!-- STATUS: DONE -->

Sexual behavior is how people experience and express their sexuality. It is one of the most universal of human activities and also one of the most varied. What counts as sex, who does it, how often, and in what ways all shift across time, culture, age, ability, and personal taste. Sexual behavior may or may not lead to orgasm, and it may or may not be **procreative**, meaning intended to produce a pregnancy. In fact, the overwhelming majority of human sexual activity is non-procreative. People spend far more of their sexual lives seeking pleasure, closeness, stress relief, and self-expression than they spend trying to conceive. That single fact tells you most of what this chapter is about.

The behaviors covered here range from the solo to the partnered, and from the very common to the less so. We start with **masturbation**, the most reliable sexual partner most people will ever have, then move through oral sex, intercourse and the positions people use, anal sex, and the toys and lubricants that round out the menu. From there we turn to orgasm itself, including the well-documented gap between how often men and women reach it and what can be done to close that gap. We end with a frank look at what happens when sex becomes difficult, because that is part of nearly everyone's sexual life at some point too.

A note before we begin. This chapter describes behaviors in plain terms because that is the most useful way to learn about them. Naming things accurately is not the same as endorsing or recommending them, and nothing here is a prescription for what your own sex life should look like. The healthiest sexual repertoire is the one that is consensual, safe, and satisfying to the people involved, whatever it happens to include.

## 9.1 Masturbation

<!-- STATUS: DONE -->

**L.O. 9.1 Describe how and why people masturbate, including common techniques and shifting cultural attitudes.**

**Masturbation** is the act of stimulating one's own genitals for pleasure. It is sometimes called self-pleasure, and it is almost certainly the most common partnered-or-solo sexual behavior on the planet, with the partner being oneself. People masturbate to feel good, to relax, to fall asleep, to relieve stress, to explore what their bodies respond to, and sometimes simply out of boredom or habit. Because it carries no risk of pregnancy and no risk of sexually transmitted infection, it is also the safest sex a person can have.

For a behavior this ordinary, masturbation has been remarkably well hidden. People do it far more than they admit, and the result is a wide gap between how common it actually is and how comfortable people are talking about it. National surveys consistently find that most adults masturbate, that men report doing so more often than women, and that the numbers have been creeping upward over time as attitudes loosen. In Britain's national survey, about 78% of men and 40% of women reported masturbating in the previous month, figures that have risen across successive surveys, with the largest recent increases among the youngest adults (Fischer et al., 2025). Large U.S. surveys find a similar pattern across nearly the entire lifespan, from adolescence into old age (Herbenick et al., 2010a). The gender gap is real, but part of it reflects what people are willing to disclose rather than what they actually do, since women face more cultural pressure to stay quiet about it.

> **FUN FACT**
> Humans did not invent masturbation, and we are far from the only species that does it. By compiling the largest dataset on the behavior ever assembled, researchers traced masturbation back through the primate family tree to a common ancestor of all monkeys and apes, meaning the behavior is at least 40 million years old (Brindle et al., 2023). They also found it may be adaptive rather than wasteful: for males, masturbating can raise arousal before mating and help clear out older, lower-quality sperm, leaving fresher sperm for reproduction.

### 9.1.1 How People Masturbate

There is no single correct technique, and most people refine their own through trial and error. That said, some patterns are common enough to describe.

Men typically masturbate by wrapping the fingers around the shaft of the penis and stroking up and down, with much of the sensation concentrated at the **glans**, the highly sensitive head of the penis. Men who are uncircumcised can use the movement of the **foreskin** to stimulate the glans, while circumcised men more often use a lubricant to reduce friction. Because the glans is so sensitive, focusing stimulation there tends to bring orgasm on quickly, whereas shifting attention to the shaft can slow things down. Some men use this deliberately as a way to build control and last longer during partnered sex.

Women's masturbation tends to center on the **clitoris**, the small, densely nerve-packed organ at the top of the vulva whose only known job is pleasure. Many women work from the outside in, beginning with broad, indirect touch around the vulva and clitoral hood and moving toward more direct clitoral stimulation as arousal builds, since the clitoris can feel almost too sensitive when touched directly early on. Common techniques include rubbing or circling the clitoris with the fingers, varying speed and pressure, and adding vaginal stimulation, which for many women feels best at higher levels of arousal. Vibrators are widely used, and we return to them later in the chapter.

### 9.1.2 Shifting Attitudes Toward Masturbation

Given how common and harmless masturbation is, the historical reaction to it looks bizarre in hindsight. For much of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Western medicine treated **"self-abuse,"** as it was tellingly called, as a genuine disease that could cause blindness, insanity, acne, infertility, and a long list of other ailments. The panic shaped daily life in ways that still echo today. The breakfast cereals invented by Dr. John Harvey Kellogg and the crackers named for Sylvester Graham were both promoted as bland foods meant to dampen sexual urges and discourage the habit. None of these supposed harms were real.

Modern medicine takes the opposite view. Masturbation is now understood as a normal and healthy part of sexual life at every age. It is linked to better sleep, lower stress, and relief from menstrual cramps, and it gives people a low-stakes way to learn what they enjoy, which tends to make partnered sex better. It is also not just for single people. Plenty of people in happy, sexually active relationships masturbate, and doing so is not a sign that anything is wrong. The lingering shame around the behavior is a leftover from a much older and far less accurate way of thinking about sex.

## 9.2 Oral Sex

<!-- STATUS: DONE -->

**L.O. 9.2 Describe the behaviors involved in oral sex.**

**Oral sex** is the use of the mouth, lips, and tongue to stimulate a partner's genitals. It has a few specific names depending on the anatomy involved. **Cunnilingus** is oral stimulation of the vulva and clitoris. **Fellatio** is oral stimulation of the penis. **Anilingus**, sometimes called rimming, is oral stimulation of the anus. Oral sex can be a warm-up to other activities, the main event, or an end in itself, and for many couples it is a regular part of their sexual routine rather than a special occasion.

It is also extremely common. In national U.S. data, the large majority of adults report having given and received oral sex at some point in their lives, and more than half of women and men between 18 and 49 report doing so in a given year (Herbenick et al., 2010a). One survey found that about 59% of Americans under 30, and roughly 84% of those 30 and older, had received oral sex at some point (Reece et al., 2015). In short, oral sex is a mainstream behavior, not a fringe one.

The basic techniques are straightforward, though doing them well depends far more on attention and communication than on any secret move. In cunnilingus, a partner uses the tongue and lips to stimulate the clitoris and the surrounding labia, often with a steady, consistent rhythm, since erratic changes in pace tend to interrupt arousal right as it builds. In fellatio, a partner uses the lips and tongue on the glans and shaft of the penis, where the glans and the underside near the head are most sensitive. In both cases, the person on the receiving end is the best available guide, and a quick word about what feels good is worth more than any amount of guessing.

One pattern is worth flagging because it connects to a theme later in the chapter. In heterosexual encounters, fellatio tends to happen more often than cunnilingus, meaning women, on average, receive oral sex less often than they give it. That imbalance matters, because oral sex is one of the most reliable routes to orgasm for women, a point we return to in the section on the orgasm gap.

> **FUN FACT**
> Oral sex is not just popular, it is strategically useful for closing the orgasm gap. In a study of more than 50,000 adults, women were far more likely to reach orgasm in encounters that included oral sex along with other forms of stimulation than in encounters built around intercourse alone (Frederick et al., 2018). Variety, it turns out, is not just the spice of life. It is also the statistics of orgasm.

## 9.3 Coitus and Sexual Positions

<!-- STATUS: DONE -->

**L.O. 9.3 Compare the four most common sexual positions and explain how each applies to partners of different genders.**

**Coitus**, also called penile-vaginal intercourse or PIV, is the insertion of the penis into the vagina. It is the behavior most people picture when they hear the word "sex," and in survey after survey it remains the most commonly reported partnered behavior among adults. It is worth remembering, though, that it is one item on a long menu, not the whole meal, and that plenty of satisfying sex involves no coitus at all.

A quick note on names. Sex positions tend to have two sets of labels: clinical ones, like "receiving partner on top," and the everyday names people actually use, like cowgirl. This book uses the everyday names because they are the ones you will hear in real life. Just keep in mind that the names are about bodies and angles, not about who is doing what. The "person on top" in the cowgirl position can be a man, the receiving partner in any position can be of any gender, and every position described below works for same-sex couples as readily as for different-sex couples, with or without a strap-on or toy standing in for a penis. What follows are the four most common positions and the practical trade-offs of each.

**Missionary** places the receiving partner on their back with the penetrating partner facing them from above. Its great advantage is intimacy. The face-to-face arrangement makes eye contact, kissing, and talking easy, and it leaves hands free to stimulate the clitoris, nipples, and other areas. The main drawbacks are that it gives the partner on the bottom relatively little control over depth and pace, and that the angle does not provide much direct clitoral contact on its own.

**Cowgirl**, sometimes called cowboy or rider, reverses this, with the receiving partner on top. This position hands control of depth, angle, speed, and rhythm to the partner on top, which is a big deal: when people can steer their own stimulation, they tend to reach orgasm more reliably. Either partner can easily reach the clitoris, and the position generally allows the penetrating partner to last longer. The main downside is physical effort, since the partner on top carries the work and may tire in the hips and thighs.

**Doggystyle**, or rear entry, has the penetrating partner enter from behind while the receiving partner is on hands and knees. It allows deep penetration and lets the receiving partner adjust depth by tilting the pelvis, and the angle stimulates the front wall of the vagina well. The trade-offs are that the clitoris is harder to reach and may need a free hand or toy, that deep penetration can bump the cervix and cause discomfort, and that air can move in and out of the vagina, producing a sound sometimes called queefing. That last one is harmless, common, and not worth any embarrassment.

**Spooning** has both partners lying on their sides, usually with the penetrating partner behind, fitted together like two spoons in a drawer. Its main virtue is comfort. It takes little effort, reduces strain and fatigue, and works well during pregnancy, with limited mobility, or simply when both partners are tired. In the rear-entry version, the clitoris is easy to reach. The cost of all that ease is leverage: neither partner has much room to control depth or pace.

If this seems like a short list, that is by design. Magazines love to publish catalogs of fifty or more positions, but most people return again and again to a small handful of comfortable favorites, often improvised variations on the four above. The useful insight is not memorizing an acrobatic repertoire. It is noticing that position changes the angle of stimulation and who can reach the clitoris, and that this directly affects pleasure and the likelihood of orgasm, a theme we pick up shortly.

People enjoy anal sex for both anatomical and emotional reasons. For men and others with a prostate, the appeal is largely physical. The **prostate** sits just inside the body against the front wall of the rectum, and pressure on it through that wall is a reliable source of pleasure, sometimes intense enough to produce orgasm on its own. In one mapping study, about 39% of men reported being able to orgasm from receptive anal stimulation alone, compared with about 19% of women (Zaliznyak et al., 2025). For women, the pleasure tends to come from nearby structures, since the internal "legs" of the clitoris and branches of the pudendal nerve run close to the rectum, which is why many women find anal stimulation most enjoyable when it is paired with clitoral stimulation. On top of the physical, many couples are drawn to anal sex for reasons of intimacy, trust, and novelty, and the fact that it carries no risk of pregnancy.

It is also genuinely not for everyone, and that is completely fine. The single most important point is this: anal sex should not hurt. Mild unfamiliarity is one thing, but if penetration is consistently painful, that is the body's signal to stop, not a problem to push through. Unlike the vagina, the anus does not produce its own lubrication, and the tissue lining the rectum is thin and tears easily. That combination is why comfort and safety go hand in hand here.

A few practices make anal sex both more comfortable and safer:

- **Go slowly and start small.** Beginning with a finger or a small toy lets the ring of muscle around the anus, which is normally held tight, relax gradually.
- **Use plenty of lubricant, and reapply.** Because the area does not self-lubricate, generous lube is not optional. It reduces friction, pain, and the risk of small tears.
- **Pair it with other stimulation.** Combining anal play with clitoral or penile stimulation makes the experience more pleasurable and helps the body relax and associate the sensation with pleasure.
- **Mind hygiene and infection risk.** Some people clear the bowels or use a gentle anal douche beforehand for peace of mind, though this is optional. More importantly, the thin rectal lining makes anal sex the highest-risk sexual behavior for transmitting HIV and other infections, so condoms meaningfully lower that risk. Anything that has been in the anus, including a penis, finger, or toy, should be washed or re-covered before it touches the vagina, since this is a common route for bacterial infection.

## 9.5 Sex Toys and Lubricants

<!-- STATUS: DONE -->

**L.O. 9.5 Identify common sex toys and lubricants and their functions.**

People have always used props to enhance sex, and today the options range from the basic to the wildly inventive. Two categories cover most of what people actually use: lubricants and a small set of common toys.

### 9.5.1 Lubricants

A **lubricant**, or lube, is a slippery substance applied to the genitals to reduce friction during sex. It is the single most useful and most underused product in this entire section, and it deserves to be discussed first. The large majority of women report that using lubricant makes sex feel both more pleasurable and more comfortable (Herbenick et al., 2011), and most American women have used it at some point (Herbenick et al., 2014).

Lube earns its keep in several ways. It increases pleasure by reducing friction, it eases or prevents the discomfort and small tears that can come from prolonged activity or insufficient natural lubrication, and it is essential for anal sex, where the body provides no lubrication of its own. It also addresses a common and widely misunderstood issue: vaginal dryness. Dryness is not just a feature of older age. It can result from hormonal shifts, stress, dehydration, breastfeeding, certain medications, and hormonal birth control, and it says nothing about whether a person is aroused or attracted to their partner. Reaching for lube is a practical fix, not an admission of a problem.

One safety point matters here. Lubricants come in water-based, silicone-based, and oil-based varieties, and the type matters when condoms are involved. Oil-based lubricants, including lotions and petroleum jelly, break down latex and can cause a condom to fail. Only water-based and silicone-based lubricants are safe to use with latex condoms. When in doubt, water-based is the all-purpose choice.

### 9.5.2 Common Sex Toys

A **sex toy** is any device designed to be used for sexual stimulation, alone or with a partner. A handful are common enough to be worth describing.

- **Dildos** are firm, often phallic objects, usually made of silicone or rubber, used to penetrate the vagina or anus. They can be handheld or attached to a harness as a **strap-on**, which lets a partner of any gender provide penetration. Strap-ons are used by couples of all kinds, including partners where one cannot reliably maintain an erection.
- **Butt plugs** are tapered toys designed to be inserted into the anus and held in place. The single most important feature of any anal toy is a **flared base**, the wide stopper at the bottom. Unlike the vagina, the rectum can draw objects upward and inward, so a flared base is what keeps a toy from getting pulled in and lost, which is a genuine and very preventable trip to the emergency room.
- **Vibrators** are devices that deliver vibration for stimulation, most often of the clitoris but also internally or on the penis. They are the most popular sex toy by a wide margin. About half of American women report having used one, and far from being a problem, vibrator use is associated with better sexual function across the board, including desire, arousal, and ease of orgasm (Herbenick et al., 2009). They do not "ruin" sensitivity or replace partners, two common worries that the research does not support.
- **Pocket vaginas**, also called masturbator sleeves, are soft, hollow toys designed to be used on the penis during masturbation. They are essentially the mirror image of a dildo, and come in a range of textures and shapes.
- **Cock rings** fit around the base of the penis to help maintain a firmer erection for longer, and some include a small attached vibrator to stimulate a partner's clitoris during intercourse.

> **FUN FACT**
> If you can imagine it, someone is probably selling it. The sex toy industry now includes **teledildonics**, internet-connected devices that a partner can control remotely from anywhere in the world, which has changed what long-distance relationships can look like. One genuinely important safety note from this gadget age: metal sex toys, like any metal in or on the body, must come out before an MRI. The machine is essentially a giant magnet, and it does not care what the metal is attached to.

## 9.6 Maximizing Pleasure

**L.O. 9.6 Analyze the psychology of orgasm, the orgasm gap, and strategies for maximizing pleasure.**

So far this chapter has been a tour of what people do. This section is about how to make those things feel as good as they can, which for most people eventually leads to a conversation about orgasm. For many people, orgasm is the part of the cycle they are most curious about and most misinformed about. Let us look at what actually happens, how much it varies, and which popular beliefs do not hold up.

### 9.6.1 What Happens During Orgasm

Orgasm is the brief, intense peak of sexual arousal. It centers on a series of rhythmic muscle contractions in the pelvic floor, especially the pubococcygeus muscle. On average there are about eight to ten contractions, roughly 0.8 seconds apart, so a typical orgasm lasts a little under ten seconds. Although the contractions start in the genital area, the pleasure often radiates outward through the body. Breathing, heart rate, and blood pressure all peak at this moment. One striking finding is that when men and women are asked to describe orgasm in writing, their descriptions are nearly impossible to tell apart. The subjective experience seems remarkably similar across sexes.

There are some physical differences in the plumbing. In men, orgasm is usually, though not always, accompanied by emission and then ejaculation, the release of semen through the urethra. Some women also release fluid during orgasm from the paraurethral glands. A long-running debate concerns whether orgasms from clitoral stimulation differ from those involving the G-spot. Masters and Johnson argued they were essentially the same, since both engage the same outer vaginal muscles. Later research complicated that view, finding that clitoral orgasms tend to be felt mainly in the clitoris while G-spot orgasms are more often felt throughout the body, with deeper blood flow in the vagina (Komisaruk et al., 2006; Jannini et al., 2014). And the clitoris and G-spot are not the only routes. Some people reach orgasm from stimulation of the nipples, lips, or other areas, some during sleep, and a few women can reach orgasm from mental imagery alone (Whipple et al., 1992; Komisaruk & Whipple, 2011).

The brain at orgasm is a spectacle. Brain imaging shows that the regions lighting up during orgasm overlap with those activated by a heroin rush, including deep pleasure and reward centers that run on dopamine (Komisaruk & Whipple, 2005; Komisaruk et al., 2004; Gabry, 2008). At the same time, activity in the thinking part of the brain quiets down, which fits the common experience of orgasm as a moment when the mind goes briefly blank. A surge of oxytocin, sometimes nicknamed the bonding hormone, helps drive the muscle contractions and is thought to encourage closeness with a partner afterward.

After orgasm comes resolution. Blood pressure, heart rate, and breathing return to baseline. Erections subside and the swelling of vasocongestion fades, usually over about fifteen minutes, often with a wave of relaxation. In men who have ejaculated, there is typically a refractory period, from roughly thirty seconds to well over an hour, during which another orgasm is physically difficult or impossible. Its length depends on age and health. Interestingly, a new and highly arousing partner can shorten the refractory period, an effect known as the Coolidge effect.

### 9.6.2 Diversity in the Response Cycle

The phases described above are typical, but they are not a script everyone follows. There is enormous variation in how individuals move through the cycle. Some people repeat phases, some skip them, and the time spent in each phase differs widely. One person might move smoothly from excitement to a single orgasm and into resolution. Another might experience waves of rising and dipping arousal before climax, or reach a plateau of high arousal without orgasm at all, then return slowly to baseline. None of these patterns is more correct than the others. They are simply different, and all are normal.

### 9.6.3 Myths About Orgasm

Orgasm may be the single most myth-laden topic in human sexuality. Here are five beliefs worth correcting.

Myth: Women are slow and men are fast. During penetrative sex, the penis usually receives more direct stimulation than the clitoris, so in that specific situation men often do reach orgasm faster. But that is about the type of stimulation, not the person. When the clitoris is stimulated directly, through a vibrator or oral sex, women tend to climax just as quickly as men, and are actually more likely to go on to additional orgasms.

Myth: Only women can have multiple orgasms. It is easier for women to have multiple orgasms, but it is not impossible for men. The barrier for men is ejaculation, which triggers the refractory period. Orgasms that are not accompanied by ejaculation, which some men can learn with practice, do not trigger that same recovery period. Multiple orgasms in men therefore usually take the form of several non-ejaculatory orgasms followed by a final one with ejaculation (Wibowo & Wassersug, 2016). It is also worth saying that not all women have multiple orgasms. Some have one, some have several, and some never experience orgasm at all.

Myth: Only women fake orgasms. Women are more likely to fake, but men do it too. In one study, about half of women and a quarter of men reported having faked an orgasm (Goodman et al., 2017). Faking happens across all kinds of sexual activity. The most common reasons people gave were that orgasm seemed unlikely or was taking too long, that they wanted the encounter to end, or that they did not want to hurt a partner's feelings. The researchers noted that the responses hinted at a cultural script in which women are expected to orgasm first and men are held responsible for making it happen.

Myth: A woman who does not orgasm from intercourse just cannot let go. This belief unfairly frames a common experience as a personal or emotional failing. In fact, a woman's likelihood of orgasm during penetrative sex is substantially shaped by anatomy and genetics. A twin study found that about a third of women rarely or never orgasm from intercourse, and that genes account for a large share of the variation (Dunn et al., 2005). One key factor is simply how close the clitoris sits to the vaginal opening, which affects how much it gets stimulated during intercourse. This is biology, not a lack of effort or willingness.

Myth: Only men get blue balls. The familiar ache some men feel after arousal without orgasm comes from blood that built up in the genitals during arousal and has not yet dissipated. But this is not unique to men. The genitals of people with vulvas also become engorged through vasocongestion, and the same unrelieved swelling can cause discomfort. If anything, blood can take longer to clear from the vulva than from the testicles, so the experience can be at least as uncomfortable (Wellings et al., 1994).

### 9.6.4 The Orgasm Gap

Those myths point to a larger and well-documented pattern called the **orgasm gap**: in heterosexual sex, men reach orgasm far more often than women. In a U.S. national survey of more than 50,000 adults, about 95% of heterosexual men reported usually or always reaching orgasm during partnered sex, compared with only about 65% of heterosexual women (Frederick et al., 2018).

The most revealing number in that same study is a different one. Lesbian women reported orgasming about 86% of the time, far closer to the men's rate than to the rate for heterosexual women. That single comparison undoes the old assumption that women's bodies are simply harder to satisfy. If the gap were a matter of anatomy, it would not nearly vanish the moment the partner is another woman. The body is not the problem. What happens during sex is.

The main culprit is a cultural script that treats intercourse as the main event and everything else as "foreplay," a warm-up to the real thing. That script suits the penis, which receives direct, rhythmic stimulation during intercourse. It serves the clitoris poorly, because the clitoris usually gets very little stimulation from penetration alone, and the clitoris is where the large majority of women's orgasms come from. Stacked on top of the anatomy are several psychological and cultural factors: sex education and media rarely mention the clitoris, so many people of all genders simply know less about it; many women learn to prioritize a partner's orgasm over their own; and sex is routinely treated as finished the moment the man ejaculates, which quietly writes the woman's orgasm out of the script.

> **FUN FACT**
> The clitoris is built for exactly one job. It is the only organ in the human body whose sole known purpose is pleasure. A 2023 study that actually counted the nerve fibers found roughly 10,280 of them in the external glans of the clitoris, well above the "8,000" figure repeated for decades, and more than are packed into the far larger head of the penis (Uloko et al., 2023). It is a remarkable amount of sensory hardware to leave unaddressed, which is more or less the story of the orgasm gap.

### 9.6.5 Closing the Orgasm Gap

The good news follows directly from the bad news. Because the gap is mostly about behavior and belief rather than biology, it is largely fixable, and the research is unusually clear about how. The same national survey found that women were far more likely to orgasm when their encounters included a wider range of acts, especially ones that stimulate the clitoris (Frederick et al., 2018). A handful of strategies do most of the work:

- **Center clitoral stimulation, not just penetration.** Oral sex, manual stimulation, vibrators, and intercourse positions that allow the clitoris to be touched all raise the odds of orgasm dramatically. Intercourse and clitoral stimulation are not competitors; they work best together.
- **Combine acts and take more time.** Longer encounters and a mix of behaviors predict women's orgasm far better than intercourse alone. The reliable combination of deep kissing, manual genital stimulation, and oral sex outperforms any single act.
- **Learn your own body first.** Women who masturbate and discover what works for them are more likely to orgasm with a partner, because they can recognize and ask for the stimulation they need.
- **Talk about it.** Telling a partner what feels good, and treating the woman's orgasm as a shared goal rather than an optional bonus, closes much of the remaining distance. Most of the gap lives in the gap between what people want and what they say.
- **Drop the finish-line script.** Sex does not have to be a single act that ends when one person climaxes. Partners can take turns, with one person's orgasm through oral or manual stimulation coming before or after intercourse rather than being squeezed out by it.

One important caveat keeps all of this in perspective. Orgasm is not the only measure of good sex, and treating it as a mandatory finish line can create exactly the performance pressure that makes it harder to reach. Plenty of satisfying, intimate sex involves no orgasm at all. The point of naming the orgasm gap is not to add one more thing to worry about. It is to retire the false idea that some bodies just cannot, and to replace it with something more accurate and more useful: pleasure is a skill, and skills can be learned.

## 9.7 When Sex Is Difficult

<!-- STATUS: DONE -->

**L.O. 9.7 Describe common sexual difficulties and the ways they are treated.**

Up to this point, the chapter has mostly described sex working the way people hope it will. It does not always. Sexual difficulties are common, they are usually treatable, and nearly everyone runs into at least one at some point in their life. An off night, a stressful week, or a new and nerve-racking partner can throw any of these systems off temporarily, and that is simply being human, not having a disorder. The line between a passing frustration and a genuine problem is drawn at two features: the difficulty is persistent, and it causes real distress. This section covers four of the most common difficulties, two that show up more often in men and two that show up more often in women, along with how each is treated. The most important takeaway is the one that shame tends to bury: these problems are widespread and very fixable, and the main obstacle to fixing them is usually the embarrassment that keeps people from asking for help.

### 9.7.1 Premature Ejaculation and Erectile Dysfunction

**Premature ejaculation** is the most common sexual difficulty in men, especially in men under 40. It refers to ejaculation that consistently happens sooner than a person wants, with little control over the timing, in a way that causes distress. Clinicians often mark it at around one minute from penetration for the lifelong form, but the more useful definition is simply that it happens earlier than the person and their partner would like, often enough to be a problem. Estimates vary widely, but a frequently cited figure is that around 30% of men experience it (Laumann et al., 1999). The causes are often psychological, including anxiety, overarousal, and the heightened excitement of a new partner, though physical factors can contribute. The good news is that it responds well to treatment. Behavioral techniques like the start-stop and squeeze methods train greater control by repeatedly approaching the point of climax and backing off. Certain antidepressants in the SSRI family delay ejaculation as a side effect and are sometimes prescribed for that purpose, and topical numbing products or thicker condoms can reduce sensitivity enough to help.

**Erectile dysfunction**, or ED, is the recurring inability to get or keep an erection firm enough for satisfying sex. Unlike premature ejaculation, it becomes more common with age, rising from roughly 2% of men under 30 to nearly half of men over 70. That age pattern is a useful clue to cause. In older men, ED more often has a physical basis, such as reduced blood flow, diabetes, or heart disease, which is why a new case in an older man is worth a medical checkup. In younger men, it is far more often psychological, driven by performance anxiety, stress, or simple nervousness, and an occasional episode is completely normal and not a sign of anything wrong. Treatments are effective and well known. Medications in the PDE-5 inhibitor family, such as sildenafil (sold as Viagra), increase blood flow to the penis and work for most men. The vacuum erection devices mentioned earlier in the chapter draw blood into the penis mechanically, and addressing the underlying cause, whether anxiety or a health condition, often resolves the problem at its root.

### 9.7.2 Painful Sex and Low Desire

For women, two of the most common difficulties are pain and a loss of interest. The single most important thing to know about **painful sex** is that it is never something a person is simply supposed to endure. Pain is a signal with a cause, and causes can be addressed. Doctors use the term **dyspareunia** for recurrent genital pain during sex, and it affects an estimated 10% to 20% of women at some point, with rates often higher among younger women who are newer to sexual activity. A related condition, **vaginismus**, involves the involuntary tightening of the muscles around the vaginal opening, sometimes so strongly that penetration becomes impossible. It affects a smaller share of women, on the order of a few percent, and is frequently tied to anxiety, fear, or past negative or traumatic experiences, though the body's reaction is automatic and not a matter of willpower. Both conditions respond to treatment. Depending on the cause, that may include lubricants, pelvic floor physical therapy, vaginal dilators that gradually and gently build comfort with penetration, treating any underlying medical issue, and counseling that reduces the anxiety feeding the cycle of pain.

**Low sexual desire** is the most common sexual difficulty reported by women, though men experience it too. When a persistent lack of interest in sex causes a person genuine distress, clinicians call it **hypoactive sexual desire disorder**, with prevalence estimates generally falling between about 5% and 14%. The causes are wide-ranging and often have little to do with the genitals: stress, exhaustion, relationship conflict, depression, hormonal shifts, and the side effects of common medications, especially SSRI antidepressants, can all flatten desire. One distinction matters here. Two partners simply wanting sex at different frequencies is extremely common and is not a disorder; it is a mismatch to be negotiated, not a disease to be cured. When low desire is genuinely distressing, treatment focuses on the underlying contributors, sometimes with the help of therapy, adjustments to medication, or, less commonly, prescription drugs developed specifically for the condition.

> **FUN FACT**
> People have hunted for a magic libido switch for thousands of years, and the search has produced a museum of failures. The word **aphrodisiac** comes from Aphrodite, the Greek goddess of love, but most famous candidates do not survive scientific scrutiny. Oysters and chocolate show little real effect beyond what people expect them to have, which means the active ingredient is mostly the placebo effect. "Spanish fly," the most notorious of them, is actively dangerous, a toxic substance made from blister beetles that can damage the kidneys and gut. A few plant extracts like ginseng and maca show modest promise in small studies, but the honest summary is that the most reliable aphrodisiacs are still the unglamorous ones: low stress, good sleep, and a partner you are genuinely into.

## 9.8 Summary

<!-- STATUS: DONE -->

Sexual behavior is how people express their sexuality, and for the vast majority of human history and human lives it has been about pleasure, connection, and self-expression far more than reproduction. This chapter surveyed the range of those behaviors.

Masturbation is the most common sexual behavior of all, practiced by the large majority of adults, and despite a bizarre history of medical panic, it is now understood as normal, healthy, and useful for learning one's own body. Oral sex, including cunnilingus and fellatio, is a mainstream behavior that the majority of adults engage in, and it is one of the most reliable routes to orgasm for women. Coitus remains the most commonly reported partnered behavior, and the four most common positions, missionary, cowgirl, doggystyle, and spooning, each offer different trade-offs in intimacy, control, and clitoral access. Anal sex is more common than many assume, and its appeal is anatomical as well as emotional, though comfort and safety depend on going slowly, using plenty of lubricant, and respecting that pain is a signal to stop. Lubricants and a small set of sex toys, especially vibrators, round out the everyday sexual menu.

The chapter then turned from behaviors to pleasure itself. Orgasm is a brief, full-body peak whose subjective experience is remarkably similar across sexes, though it varies enormously from person to person and is wrapped in more myths than almost any other topic in human sexuality. The orgasm gap, the large difference in how often men and women reach orgasm in heterosexual sex, turns out to be a product of cultural scripts and stimulation rather than anatomy, which means it is largely fixable through clitoral focus, variety, self-knowledge, and communication. Finally, sexual difficulties, including premature ejaculation, erectile dysfunction, painful sex, and low desire, are common and usually treatable, and the biggest barrier to addressing them is the shame that keeps people from seeking help that works.
